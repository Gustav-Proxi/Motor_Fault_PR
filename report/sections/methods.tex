\section{Data and Methods}\label{sec:methods}

\textbf{Data.} We use the smartphone vibration dataset of \citet{ertargin2026}: an iPhone 15 Pro Max lies on a squirrel-cage induction motor and logs
six inertial channels at 100\,Hz, the raw accelerations $g_X, g_Y, g_Z$ and the
gravity-compensated user accelerations $g^{u}_X, g^{u}_Y, g^{u}_Z$. There are six health states:
healthy (H), insufficient lubrication (B1), severely insufficient lubrication (B2), a cracked
outer bearing ring (B3), supply-voltage imbalance (V) and a broken rotor bar (R). Each class is
recorded once at each of six operating conditions (supply frequency 30, 40 or 50\,Hz, unloaded
or loaded), giving 36 recordings of about 15 minutes each. The 100\,Hz rate puts the Nyquist
frequency at 50\,Hz, below the textbook signatures of B3 (outer-race defect frequency
44--86\,Hz) and V (twice line frequency, 60--100\,Hz).

\textbf{Windows.} Each recording is cut into windows of 1{,}024 samples (10.24\,s) with a
512-sample step, giving 6{,}264 windows (about 174 per recording). The mean of each window is
removed per channel, which removes gravity and sensor offset; amplitudes are not normalised,
because absolute vibration energy is discriminative, and all scaling is fitted inside the
cross-validation folds.

\textbf{Features.} Each window yields 21 hand-written features per channel, 126 in total.
Eight are time-domain statistics: RMS, variance, skewness, kurtosis, crest factor,
peak-to-peak, shape factor and impulse factor. Thirteen are spectral. The shaft speed $f_1$ is
estimated per window as the strongest peak between 0.80 and 1.03 times the synchronous speed
(half the supply frequency, for a four-pole motor); measured $f_1$ is about 15, 20 and 25\,Hz
unloaded and 4--8\% lower under load, so a fixed nominal speed would misplace every
speed-relative feature. On a full-window Hann periodogram (0.098\,Hz bins) we compute the
energy fractions at $f_1$ and $2f_1$ ($\pm$0.3\,Hz), a sideband fraction 0.5--3\,Hz either
side of $f_1$, and band powers below $0.5f_1$, around $f_1$, around $2f_1$ and above $2.5f_1$.
On a Welch estimate (256-sample segments) we compute the spectral centroid, spread and entropy,
and from a short-time Fourier transform (64-sample segments) the maximum and mean of the
spectral kurtosis. The slip $1 - f_1/f_\mathrm{sync}$ is the
last feature.

\textbf{Projections.} After standardisation, each pipeline applies one of five front-ends:
none (the 126 raw features), PCA keeping 95\% of the variance, PCA to five dimensions, Fisher
LDA to five dimensions (at most $C-1$ for six classes), or RBF Kernel PCA to five dimensions.
PCA is the eigendecomposition of the sample covariance. LDA solves the generalised
eigenproblem $S_B v = \lambda S_W v$ with the within-class scatter shrunk towards a scaled
identity by the Ledoit--Wolf estimate \cite{ledoit2004}, which keeps $S_W$ invertible when
features are nearly constant within a recording. Kernel PCA \cite{scholkopf1998} centres the RBF
kernel in feature space and keeps its top five eigenvectors; the kernel is built on at most
1{,}500 randomly chosen training windows, and every window is projected against that set. All
three projections are implemented by hand.

\textbf{Classifiers.} Each front-end feeds six classifiers: $k$-NN with Euclidean distance
($k=5$), $k$-NN after whitening (equivalent to Mahalanobis distance), a linear SVM ($C=1$), an
RBF SVM ($C=10$), a random forest of 300 trees, and Gaussian naive Bayes. This gives 30 models.
The scaler, the projection and the classifier form one pipeline, fitted on the training fold
only.
